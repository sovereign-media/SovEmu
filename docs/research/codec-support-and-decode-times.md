# Codec support and decode times

Research for sovereign-media/SovEmu#184, gathered on 2026-11-23: which video codecs SovEmu's
clients decode, what a frame takes them, which encoders a server can run, and which encoder to
add next.

How to read it:

- It was compiled from public sources by research agents and has not been checked line by line
  against the original pages, which were read through a summarising tool. Check a source before
  relying on an exact figure or quotation.
- The decode times are mostly individual users' reports, nearly all at 1080p or higher. Almost
  nothing is published at console resolutions.
- The recommendations (the order of encoders to add, and the starting estimates) are this
  report's own judgement from that evidence, and are marked as such where they appear.

## Summary: measure decoders; codec tables mislead

H.264 hardware decode and VP8 software decode are the only pair every client in scope accepts, and the public data says SovEmu should keep both as its floor, order them per device class, and add **VP9 next, AV1 after it and H.265 last**. The most useful finding is about latency rather than support: reported decode times fall into three bands (about 0.1 to 2 ms on Windows PCs and the Nvidia Shield, 2 to 6 ms on Apple silicon Macs, and anywhere from 4 to 35 ms on MediaTek and Amlogic Android devices), and the slow figures cluster near 16, 23 and 30 ms regardless of resolution, bitrate or codec, which is frame buffering in the decoder driver and not slow silicon. SovEmu's own 23 ms on an Amlogic S7D sits exactly in that cluster, so a hardware decoder that merely accepts a low-latency request does not deserve a 4 ms estimate on Amlogic or MediaTek, and no public evidence shows that H.265 fixes it on the same chips. On the encode side, the Alder Lake server hardware-encodes H.264, H.265 and VP9 but not AV1; VP9 hardware encode exists on Intel alone, H.265 on every vendor, AV1 only on 2022-and-later hardware. The public record is thin in the places that matter most to SovEmu: almost no decode figures below 1080p, none for iPhone, iPad, Realtek, Broadcom or VP9 hardware decoders, no per-frame hardware encode times at console resolutions, and no minimum-resolution tables for hardware encoders. Every estimate below is therefore a starting value to be replaced by the device's own measurement, which is what SovEmu already does.

## Only H.264 and VP8 reach every client; the rest are negotiated per device

RFC 7742 makes VP8 and H.264 Constrained Baseline mandatory for WebRTC browsers, and Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari all comply ([RFC 7742](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7742.txt); [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Media/Guides/Formats/WebRTC_codecs)). The two are complementary rather than redundant. **Chromium builds without proprietary codecs have no H.264 in WebRTC at all**, because libwebrtc's `rtc_use_h264` follows the proprietary-codec build switch ([webrtc.gni](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/refs/heads/main/webrtc.gni)); Fedora's Chromium failed Teams calls with "No video codecs in common" for that reason ([Red Hat bug 2264332](https://bugzilla.redhat.com/show_bug.cgi?id=2264332)). In the other direction, VP8 "has no hardware support on iOS devices", so it costs CPU and battery there, while H.264 is hardware-decoded and Safari's default ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Media/Guides/Formats/WebRTC_codecs)). The safe H.264 shape is Constrained Baseline, packetization-mode 1, parameter sets in-band: stock libwebrtc on Android advertises High profile only when the decoder name starts with `OMX.qcom.` or `OMX.Exynos.`, so MediaTek, Amlogic and Codec2-named decoders offer baseline parameters only ([MediaCodecVideoDecoderFactory.java](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/refs/heads/main/sdk/android/src/java/org/webrtc/MediaCodecVideoDecoderFactory.java)).

The table shows what each stack offers for WebRTC receive, which is a different question from what the device can play in a `<video>` element.

Three consequences follow. First, the native apps differ from the browser on the same device: stock iOS libwebrtc offers software AV1 on every iPhone but no H.265, the reverse of Safari. Second, H.265 availability in Chrome is per device and not per version, so the client's offered SDP or `RTCRtpReceiver.getCapabilities("video")` is the only reliable signal; `decodingInfo({type: "webrtc"})` reached Firefox only at 152 or 153 and has a documented false positive in Chrome, so it is a hint for `powerEfficient` at best ([Mozilla bug 1825286](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1825286); [W3C issues digest](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-archive/2023Dec/att-0004/Media_Capabilities_API_Issues_and_PRs.pdf)). Third, str0m's AV1, H.265 and VP9 paths took panic, overflow and negotiation fixes from 0.16.1 through 0.25.0, including a VP9 picture-id wrap-around that dropped frames, so a new codec wants str0m 0.25 or later ([str0m CHANGELOG](https://github.com/algesten/str0m/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). The Edge entry rests on user and community-moderator statements, not Microsoft documentation, and the versions of libwebrtc and str0m that SovEmu's apps and server actually pin were not inspected.

### Hardware decode: H.264 everywhere, H.265 and VP9 nearly, VP8 and AV1 not

The Android 16 CDD requires H.264, H.265, VP8, VP9 and AV1 decoders on handhelds but only says devices "SHOULD use a hardware VP8 codec", and Google has shipped dav1d as a software AV1 decoder to Android 12 and later since 2024 ([Android 16 CDD](https://source.android.com/docs/compatibility/16/android-16-cdd); [9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2024/04/19/android-av1-software-decoder/)).

The VP8 column carries the caveat that matters for SovEmu. No source states outright that VP8 is software on Amlogic or MediaTek TV chips; the conclusion rests on three independent absences (spec sheets, the vendor kernel driver tree, Google's Cast table). It agrees with SovEmu's own measurement, where VP8 on the S7D ran through a software decoder. The S7 and S7D family itself appears in no source found. The only published device-share numbers are for smartphones, the latest hard measurement dates from Q2 2024, and nothing comparable exists for TVs, streaming sticks or PCs.

## Slow decoders hold frames: 16, 23 and 30 ms regardless of resolution

No maintainer-published table of decode times per device exists. The best public data is user reports of Moonlight's on-screen statistic in GitHub issues: anecdotal, one device and firmware each, nearly all at 1080p or higher, and mostly H.264 or H.265. They are still comparable with SovEmu's numbers, because the definitions match. Moonlight on Android stamps each input buffer at enqueue and adds the wall-clock delta when the output buffer is dequeued, with rendering excluded ([MediaCodecDecoderRenderer.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-android/master/app/src/main/java/com/limelight/binding/video/MediaCodecDecoderRenderer.java)); libwebrtc stamps `decodeStart` before handing the frame to the decoder and takes the difference in the decoded callback, unless the decoder wrapper supplies its own figure ([generic_decoder.cc](https://chromium.googlesource.com/external/webrtc/+/refs/heads/main/modules/video_coding/generic_decoder.cc)). Both are submit-to-output latency: for an asynchronous hardware decoder they include every frame the decoder holds, and for a synchronous software decoder such as libvpx they are pure compute time. A decoder that holds one frame at 60 fps therefore reads at least 16.7 ms however fast the silicon is.

| Device class | Reported decode time | Conditions and source |
|---|---|---|
| Windows PCs, including five-year-old integrated graphics | **0.13 to 0.5 ms**, "sub 1 ms" | User reports, 2024 to 2026 ([moonlight-qt #1249](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-qt/issues/1249)) |
| Nvidia Shield | 1 ms; "under 5 ms" | 1440p60 HDR; user reports 2019 and 2021 ([moonlight-android #689](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-android/issues/689)) |
| Apple silicon Macs | **2 to 3 ms** at 1440p and 4K; 5 to 6 ms at 4K 144 Hz; AV1 about 3 ms on M3 Pro | VideoToolbox ([moonlight-qt #1249](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-qt/issues/1249)) |
| Intel MacBook Pro (2020) | 5.6 to 6.3 ms on macOS, 0.39 to 1.8 ms on Windows, same machine | H.265, 2K, 60 fps ([moonlight-qt #1249](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-qt/issues/1249)) |
| MediaTek, fast path working | 3 ms (Sony TV), 4 to 6 ms (Fire TV Stick 4K Gen 1), 6 to 8 ms (Helio G99) | ([moonlight-android #1241](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-android/issues/1241); [#689](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-android/issues/689)) |

| LG webOS TVs | About 30 ms at 1080p, 70 ms at 2160p | Whole video latency, not decode alone ([moonlight-tv FAQ](https://github.com/mariotaku/moonlight-tv/wiki/FAQs)) |

Moonlight's maintainer names the cause: some decoder drivers support a low-latency mode, found through a profile or undocumented parameters, and others hold output frames until more input arrives, so Fire TV decode time is "a hardware limitation" ([moonlight-android #689](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-android/issues/689)). The evidence fits buffering rather than slow computation: figures on affected devices do not move with resolution, and on MediaTek tablets and Macs the reported time drops when frames arrive faster. That second effect favours SovEmu, since a steady 60 fps emulator stream keeps the decoder fed where a desktop capture does not.

Android's standard low-latency mode does not settle the question. It arrived in Android 11, is implemented by each SoC vendor's driver, is not stated to be mandatory, and the CDD makes minimum codec latency a SHOULD ([AOSP](https://source.android.com/docs/core/media/low-latency-media); [Android 16 CDD](https://source.android.com/docs/compatibility/16/android-16-cdd)). Moonlight therefore trusts `KEY_LOW_LATENCY` alone only when the decoder advertises `FEATURE_LowLatency`, and otherwise layers MediaTek's `vdec-lowlatency`, Qualcomm's operating rate and vendor keys such as Amlogic's `vendor.low-latency.enable` ([MediaCodecHelper.java](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-android/blob/master/app/src/main/java/com/limelight/binding/video/MediaCodecHelper.java)). No source confirms that the Amlogic key lowers latency on any named device, and the same file records `vdec-lowlatency` breaking one Xiaomi TV box while being required on a Fire TV. SovEmu's 23 ms on the S7D with low latency requested is the published pattern, not an outlier.

Codec choice does not rescue a buffering decoder on the evidence available. H.264 and H.265 both read about 30 ms on the Fire TV Stick 4K and on the Dimensity 9000 ([moonlight-android #689](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-android/issues/689); [#1241](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-android/issues/1241)), and Moonlight's lists show the faster codec differing by device, with H.264 lower latency on the Chromecast with Google TV and H.265 allowed on other Amlogic boxes ([MediaCodecHelper.java](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-android/blob/master/app/src/main/java/com/limelight/binding/video/MediaCodecHelper.java)). **No VP9 hardware decode latency figure exists for any streaming client**, because Moonlight streams only H.264, H.265 and AV1.

Software decode is thinner still. The one recent ARM data set is a hobbyist benchmark on an overclocked Raspberry Pi 4 (Cortex-A72), run flat out into a null sink: libvpx VP8 at 720p reaches 240 fps (about 4.2 ms per frame), libvpx VP9 at 1080p about 115 fps (8.7 ms), dav1d AV1 at 1080p about 108 to 133 fps, and software H.264 High 10 at 1080p 73 to 78 fps ([raspberry-pi-video-codec-performance](https://github.com/DrEVILish/raspberry-pi-video-codec-performance)). These are throughput figures, and dav1d's frame threading adds delay that a real-time decoder would not accept. Scaled by pixel count, the VP8 figure implies roughly 1.3 ms at 640x448 on that core; SovEmu's measured 4 to 9 ms on a slower TV SoC is of the right order. No figures were found for Cortex-A53 or A55 cores at 480p to 720p, none for x86, none for OpenH264, and no WebRTC study or vendor post publishes a decode-stage figure per device. Academic work on cloud gaming reports network round-trip times and treats the client as a black box ([ACM, 2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3517745.3561464)).

## Default order: H.264 first, except where the decoder buffers

The estimates SovEmu uses today hold up in shape and need two corrections. Its 4 ms for a low-latency hardware decoder is conservative for PCs and right for Apple silicon. Its 6 ms for software decode at 640x448 matches its own measurement on TV-class cores. Its one-frame penalty of 16.7 ms sits at the bottom of the observed slow cluster, whose reports run 16, 18, 23, 23, 25, 30 and 25 to 35 ms, so 23 ms is the better central value. And "has a low-latency mode" should mean the decoder advertises `FEATURE_LowLatency`, not that it accepted the key, with Amlogic and MediaTek decoder names treated as buffering until measured. The table below is this report's recommendation, built from the figures above; the last column says how much data stands behind each row.

| Device class | Initial order | Hardware estimate | Software estimate | Strength of public data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Windows and Linux PC browsers | H.264, VP8 | 2 ms | 6 ms at 640x448, scaled by pixels | Good for H.264 and H.265 in a native client; none for browser WebRTC or x86 software decode |
| Chromium without proprietary codecs | VP8 only | Not applicable | As above | Build behaviour is well sourced |
| macOS, Safari and Chrome | H.264, VP8 | 4 ms | As above | Good for Macs |
| iPhone, iPad, Apple TV | H.264, VP8 | 4 ms | As above | **No figures found**; one unverified forum remark for Apple TV |
| Android phones, Qualcomm and Exynos | H.264, VP8 | 4 ms if `FEATURE_LowLatency` is advertised, else 16.7 ms | As above | Qualitative only: the maintainer calls them faster than Fire TV decoders |
| Android phones and tablets, MediaTek | H.264, VP8 | 23 ms | As above | Good, and bimodal: 3 to 8 ms or 15 to 35 ms |
| Android TV, Amlogic or MediaTek, up to about 640x480 | **VP8, then H.264** | 23 ms | 6 ms | Good for hardware; software rests on SovEmu's one data point |
| Android TV, Amlogic or MediaTek, 720p | H.264, VP8 | 23 ms | About 19 ms, scaled | Weak |
| Nvidia Shield | H.264, VP8 | 2 ms | As above | Fair |
| Realtek, Broadcom, webOS, Tizen | H.264, VP8 | 16.7 ms | As above | **None** |

The reversal at 720p on TV devices follows from what the two numbers mean. A hardware figure of 23 ms is pipeline latency and the decoder still sustains 60 fps; a software figure is compute time, and once it passes 16.7 ms the decoder falls behind. Scaling 6 ms from 640x448 to 720p by pixel count gives about 19 ms. The scaling is a rough extrapolation, decode time is not perfectly linear in pixels, and an Amazon specification page that limits VP8 to 1080p at 30 fps on a 4K stick points the same way but was seen only as a search snippet. Hardware estimates should stay flat across resolutions, since slow decoders are resolution-independent, while software estimates should scale.

Two cheap measures follow from the same sources and are inferences, not tested results. Decoders size their output delay from the bitstream, and one Qualcomm forum report found that setting the H.264 VUI `max_dec_frame_buffering` to 1 reduced a decoder's held frames ([Qualcomm developer forum](https://developer.qualcomm.com/comment/18025)), so the H.264 encoders should signal no reordering and a single reference frame. And a low decode figure does not prove low latency: the Chromecast showing 7 ms with 250 ms of perceived delay had its lag after the decoder, in power saving and TV picture processing ([moonlight-android #1352](https://github.com/moonlight-stream/moonlight-android/issues/1352)).

## The server encodes H.265 and VP9 in hardware, AV1 only in software

Intel's driver table places the i9-12900HK in its Tiger Lake to Raptor Lake column: low-power hardware encode for H.264, H.265 and VP9 at 8 and 10 bits, AV1 decode only. Both Debian driver packages keep those low-power encoders, and HuC firmware, which constant-bitrate control needs, loads by default on Alder Lake from kernel 5.17 ([intel/media-driver README](https://github.com/intel/media-driver/blob/master/README.md)). Debian 13 ships FFmpeg 7.1.5, which carries the `hevc_vaapi`, `vp9_vaapi`, `av1_vaapi`, `hevc_nvenc` and `av1_nvenc` wrappers and links libvpx, libaom, SVT-AV1 2.x, x264 and x265, but neither OpenH264 nor AMF ([FFmpeg Changelog](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/master/Changelog); [Debian libavcodec61](https://packages.debian.org/trixie/libavcodec61)).

| Codec | Intel (iHD, VA-API) | AMD (Mesa, VA-API) | Nvidia NVENC | SovEmu's server | Software in Debian 13 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| H.264 | Broadwell and later; low-power from Skylake | All VCN (2018 and later) | All | Yes | x264 |
| H.265 | Skylake and later (shader); **low-power from Ice Lake** | All VCN | GTX 10 and later | **Yes** | x265 |
| VP8 | Kaby Lake and Ice Lake shader mode only; none since | None | None | No | libvpx |
| VP9 | Ice Lake to Arrow Lake, Arc A-series, Panther Lake; **absent on Lunar Lake and Battlemage** | **None** | **None** | **Yes** | libvpx |
| AV1 | Arc A-series, Meteor Lake and later | VCN 4.0 and later (RX 7000, Phoenix) | RTX 40 and later | **No** | libaom, SVT-AV1 2.x, rav1e |

Sources: [intel/media-driver README](https://github.com/intel/media-driver/blob/master/README.md); [Wikipedia: Video Core Next](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_Core_Next); [Nvidia support matrix](https://developer.nvidia.com/video-encode-and-decode-gpu-support-matrix-new); [Debian libavcodec61](https://packages.debian.org/trixie/libavcodec61).

The pattern across vendors is plain. H.265 is the only codec newer than H.264 that all three vendors encode on pre-2022 hardware. VP9 hardware encode is an Intel feature that Nvidia and AMD never built and that Intel's own driver table drops on Lunar Lake and Battlemage, so on any other host VP9 means libvpx. AV1 is on every vendor's current generation and nothing older.

Published encode timings are scarce and none are at console resolutions. The most usable set is Chrome's own per-frame encode time on a 720p30 call in April 2025: on a Windows ThinkPad, hardware H.264 11 ms, hardware H.265 14 ms, VP8 10 ms, VP9 18 ms, libaom AV1 15 ms and OpenH264 14 ms; on a Mac mini M2 Pro, **VP8 4 ms, VP9 8 ms, AV1 6 ms**, hardware H.264 7 ms and hardware H.265 9 ms ([webrtc-developers.com](https://www.webrtc-developers.com/comparison-of-webrtc-codecs-for-video-and-screen-sharing/)). These include the browser pipeline and libwebrtc's own speed choices. Scaled by pixel count, 640x480 being one third of 720p, they imply roughly 1.3 to 3 ms for VP8, 2.7 to 6 ms for VP9 and 2 to 5 ms for libaom AV1 at 480p, and well under 1 ms for VP8 at 256x224. That scaling is an estimate: encode time carries fixed per-frame overhead and threads help less at small sizes. Realistic working figures are that real-time VP9 costs about twice VP8 and libaom AV1 somewhat less than VP9 on recent CPUs, with software VP9 or AV1 at 720p60 the configuration most likely to miss a 5 ms target.

SVT-AV1 is not yet an option from Debian's packages. Its `--rtc` mode and the M11 and M12 presets arrived in 4.0.0, where M11 runs 48% and M12 90% faster than M10 at a large quality cost, while Debian 13 links the 2.x series ([SVT-AV1 PR #3043](https://github.com/AOMediaCodec/SVT-AV1/pull/3043); [Debian libavcodec61](https://packages.debian.org/trixie/libavcodec61)). No absolute frame rate for SVT-AV1's real-time presets on any hardware was found. Software AV1 on this image means libaom in real-time mode, which is what Chrome ships.

For hardware encoders the latency controls are structural. FFmpeg's VA-API wrappers default `async_depth` to 2, described in one project's fix as leaving "a frame queued until the next arrives", and a late-2025 study at 4K60 found that quality presets moved whole-chain latency by about one frame while software encoders ran 41 to 98 frames behind against 5 to 12 for hardware ([FFmpeg encoders documentation](https://github.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/blob/release/7.1/doc/encoders.texi); [UScreen PR #45](https://github.com/majmichu1/UScreen/pull/45); [arXiv 2511.18688](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18688v2)). The same study found Intel's H.265 encoder on an Arc A770 emitting unidirectional B-frames that could not be turned off; whether Alder Lake does the same is unconfirmed. **No authoritative minimum resolution was found for any vendor's H.265, VP9 or AV1 hardware encoder**: Intel documents maxima only. Whether `vp9_vaapi` or `hevc_vaapi` accepts 256x224 on this server is the main unverified risk, and only a probe on the machine answers it.

## VP9 next: the server encodes it in hardware and every client decodes it

The public data cannot show that any of the three candidates lowers decode latency on a slow device, which narrows what a new codec is for. What remains is compression efficiency, reach, and cost on both ends. On efficiency, the one rigorous real-time comparison found (1080p, 2020) has libaom real-time AV1 saving about 9 to 13% over libvpx VP9, 18% over x264 medium and 33 to 41% over VP8 at equal VMAF, with x265 superfast beating real-time AV1 by 11 to 19% ([arXiv 2009.14165](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.14165v2)). From those figures VP9 is roughly 25 to 30% better than VP8. Against VP8 any of the three is a real step; against a good H.264 encoder the step is modest. No comparison exists on pixel-art or emulator output, and at SovEmu's bitrates on a home network the absolute saving is small, so reach and cost decide.

**VP9** is the only candidate that the current server encodes in hardware and that every client accepts without packaging work: all four browsers offer profile 0, stock libwebrtc decodes it in software on Android and iOS, and every TV SoC surveyed, Intel from Kaby Lake, all AMD VCN hardware and Nvidia from the GTX 10 series decode it in hardware. It also gives SovEmu a second software-decodable codec, considerably more efficient than VP8, for the TV devices where software decode beats a buffering hardware decoder. Its weaknesses are real: on AMD, Nvidia, Lunar Lake and Battlemage hosts it falls back to libvpx at about twice VP8's CPU cost, Apple's hardware VP9 support is undocumented per chip, and its hardware decode latency is unmeasured anywhere. On Android, libwebrtc's default factory prefers a hardware decoder with software as fallback, so VP9 on an Amlogic box would land on the hardware decoder and could buffer like H.264 does; because SovEmu builds its own app, it can route VP9 to libvpx by decoder factory when the measurement says so.

**AV1** comes second. It has the best real-time efficiency, software encoders with screen-content tools, hardware encode on every vendor's current generation and bundled dav1d decode in Chrome, Firefox 136 and later and stock libwebrtc. On this server it runs in software only, Safari offers it only on A17 Pro and M3-class hardware, and Debian's SVT-AV1 predates the real-time work. It becomes the stronger choice the day the server gains an Arc, Meteor Lake, RX 7000 or RTX 40 class encoder.

**H.265** comes last for SovEmu, against the pattern of the game-streaming products. Sunshine, GeForce NOW and Steam offer H.264, H.265 and AV1 and never VP8 or VP9, because they need hardware encode at 1080p to 4K and Nvidia and AMD have no VP9 encoder ([Sunshine configuration docs](https://docs.lizardbyte.dev/projects/sunshine/latest/md_docs_2configuration.html)). SovEmu's frames are small enough for software encoders and its clients are browsers, where H.265 is the least uniform codec: absent from Firefox, reportedly absent from Edge, hardware-only in Chrome, and missing from the upstream iOS libwebrtc factory, which makes it the option with the most client work. The devices that would receive it already hardware-decode H.264, and on the slow ones H.265 reads the same 30 ms. The strongest case for it, universal hardware encode across hosts, matters little while software VP8 and x264 already cover every host at these resolutions.

## Conclusion

The codec table is the smaller half of the answer. Support can be read from each client's SDP offer at session start, but latency cannot be read from anything a device reports: a decoder name, a hardware flag and an accepted low-latency key all coexist with 23 ms of buffering. SovEmu's design of estimating once and replacing the estimate with a measurement after one session is the right one, and the public data argues for leaning on it harder, by treating Amlogic and MediaTek hardware decoders as slow until proven fast, by letting software VP8 lead on TV devices at low resolutions, and by remembering that a decode figure says nothing about delay added after the decoder.

Adding VP9 rests on three measurements no public source provides, all of which the server can take in an afternoon: whether `vp9_vaapi` accepts 256x224 on Alder Lake, what it costs per frame including surface upload from 256x224 to 720p, and what libvpx VP9 costs in real-time mode at 480p and 720p on the 12900HK. A fourth belongs to the client: VP9 decode time on the Mi TV through both the hardware decoder and libvpx. If hardware VP9 buffers there as H.264 does and software VP9 decodes near VP8's 4 to 9 ms, VP9 has earned its place as the efficient software codec; if `vp9_vaapi` refuses small frames, the argument for it weakens to parity with software AV1, and libaom deserves the same benchmark before either is built.
